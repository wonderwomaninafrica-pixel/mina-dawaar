The Mind of an Obsessed Woman

A free sample: Chapters One and Two.

Chapter One

The woman across from me had been in love with the same unavailable man for nineteen months, and she was explaining, again, why this time was different.

Carmen sat with her coat still on. She did that when she meant to save the important thing for the last four minutes, when the coat was a way of telling herself she could still leave, right up until she said it. My office had gone gold around us, that late-May six-o’clock light this city does, coming in long over the tops of the maples on the street below so the whole room looked as if it had decided to be kind. It is the top floor of a converted house on a side street off the Danforth, mine on a long lease, shared with a periodontist on the ground floor and a woman on the second who does something quiet and lucrative with people’s feet, and at six the three practices empty out one after another and the building ticks and settles like a person taking off their shoes. I have a wait-list. I have a box of tissues on the low table that I move an inch toward people the way a bartender keeps limes within reach, and a clock the client can’t see and I can, and two chairs a careful distance apart, and Carmen sat in the far one with her coat on and told me about a text.

He had sent it at two in the morning on Saturday. Not asking for anything, she said, and she wanted me to hear that, not asking. Just that he’d been thinking about a trip they took to Prince Edward County the summer before it all went sideways, the vineyard with the goats, the terrible cheap motel with the good coffee, did she remember. And she had lain awake until the window went from black to gray holding the phone against her sternum.

“And what do you think he wanted,” I said. Not unkindly. I never say it unkindly.

“Nothing. That’s what’s different. There was no ask. He was just remembering.”

“At two in the morning.”

“People remember at two in the morning.”

They do. I let the silence sit the way you let a dressing sit before you pull it, and then I told her what a two-in-the-morning memory is, because after twenty years I can tell you what it is the way a mechanic can tell you what that sound is without lifting the hood. “He reaches for you,” I said, “when the reaching costs him nothing. Two in the morning, no one awake to see it, no promise attached to any of it. A memory is the cheapest thing he owns. It asks you for your whole night and it commits him to nothing he has to keep in the daylight. You didn’t sleep. Did he?”

“I don’t know.”

“You don’t. That’s the whole architecture of it. You gave him the entire night and you’ll never know what it cost him, and the not-knowing is the part you can’t put down, and turning it over and over is exactly the thing that keeps him warm in a life you are not in.” I moved the tissues an inch toward her. “The county was real. The goats were real. None of that is the question. The question is what you do at three on a Tuesday afternoon, when you’re the one who has to decide something and there’s no text to wait for.”

She cried a little, the contained kind, wiping under her eyes with one finger so as not to take the makeup, and then she laughed at herself, which was why I liked her. “You make it sound so simple.”

“It isn’t simple. It’s just clear. Those are different things.” I sent her home with homework, small and concrete, because large and abstract is where people go to drown. One evening this week with the phone in another room, on the far side of a closed door, charging where she’d have to stand up and cross to it. She hugged me at the door, which some of them do and some never will, and went down the stairs past the periodontist’s dark frosted glass and out into the street, and I stood in my own doorway and felt the good clean tiredness of having been useful to somebody.

Carmen was the last of nine. I want to give you the day, because the day is the thing that mattered and the day is the thing I was about to stop being able to fully inhabit, though I did not know it yet. A Tuesday. Up before the house at six, coffee made badly and drunk black, a run along the boardwalk in the flat early light because that is where I do my actual thinking, three mornings a week, not fast. Then nine hours of other people’s traps. The Bergmanns at four, married forty years and in their fortieth of not saying the thing, and I had spent fifty minutes watching a man apologize for a tone of voice while his wife catalogued, behind her eyes, the true offense from nine years back that neither of them would ever name, and what I did for the Bergmanns, week after week, was hold the space in which the unsayable thing could go on being unsaid a little more comfortably, and I told myself this was therapy and some weeks it was. A cancellation at two that gave me an hour to eat a sandwich at my desk and answer the college about a form. A new intake at eleven I was worried about and a standing client at one who was, quietly, getting well and would leave me by the fall, cured, grateful, gone, which is the whole point of them, the leaving, and never stops being a small private grief I am not allowed to have.

I will not pretend to be modest about the work, because the work is the one place I have never had to be modest. I am good at it the way some people are tall. Twenty years of sitting in a room while people describe the traps they built and then stepped into, and there is almost no trap I can’t diagram now, the tripwire and the bait and the small door that only opens inward and locks behind you with a sound like a good idea. I learned it in a walk-up on the wrong side of the Danforth with a tub in the kitchen, one course a term, a baby on each hip by the time I was twenty-two, becoming a self and a mother in the same motion so close I have never been able to separate the threads. I built this. Nobody handed it to me and no man rescued me into it, and somewhere in the building of it, without ever deciding it, I had come to believe a thing about myself that I would like now to say out loud, because it is the hinge the whole door swings on.

I believed I was safe. Not smug about it, or I’d have thought of it as belief and not fact, the way you don’t feel proud of knowing the stove is hot, you just don’t put your hand there. I had watched a thousand intelligent people walk into the fire and concluded, quietly, in the place where you keep the things you are too sensible to say, that watching was a kind of inoculation. That a woman who could see the mechanism could not be caught in it. That knowing the name of the animal was the same as being safe from its mouth. I want it on the record that I believed this completely, a licensed professional, forty years old, and that I was exactly as right about it as every person who has ever believed it, which is to say I was a woman standing very close to a stove telling herself a story about her hand.

My phone was on the desk, face down, because I don’t take it into the room with a patient. I picked it up to check the time, to see how much evening I had before I had to be a mother again, and the screen was already lit with a name.

Daniel: I found the county place. The vineyard with the goats. You’d hate it now, they put in a gift shop.

What happened was almost nothing, and the almost is a thing I did not weigh at the time and have weighed every day since. Something moved, low, a small drop and lift, the kind of thing a body does and a mind is entitled to ignore, and I ignored it, the way you’d ignore a floorboard settling in a house you have lived in for years and know to be sound. Then I read the words again and laughed, actually laughed, alone in my office, because the coincidence was funny, the county and the goats surfacing in his message an hour after they’d surfaced in Carmen’s, the whole afternoon rhyming with itself the way afternoons occasionally do, meaning nothing, the sort of thing you tell someone at dinner. I had met Daniel in February. We were friends, in the low-stakes way you are friends with a clever person you run into, a man I’d exchanged perhaps forty emails with about nothing, and if you had asked me that evening to describe the friendship I’d have called it a small good thing and moved on to the parking.

I texted him back. I did not make myself wait. There was nothing to wait for.

Mara: A gift shop is where a romance goes to be embalmed. I hope they’re at least selling goat soap.

The three dots came up, and I watched them the way you’d glance at a kettle, without weather in it, and his answer made me smile, and I put the phone in my bag and turned off the lamp and locked the office. On the drive home I thought about Sophie’s residence forms and about whether there was anything in the house to make into dinner, and I stopped for groceries and stood in the aisle deciding, and I put the mushrooms back because Eli won’t touch them and it isn’t worth the negotiation, a small domestic calculation I had made a thousand times and would go on making, and the tomatoes were next, and my hand did the thing it does, closed around one to feel the give of it before the bag, and I stopped, because it was not my thing, it was Noah’s. Noah squeezed every tomato he ever bought, gently, both hands sometimes, and had teased me for twenty years that I bought fruit like a person paying a fine, fast, eyes elsewhere. My hand had kept his habit eleven years after I kept nothing else of his, and I put the tomato in the bag and did not squeeze the next one, on purpose, correcting a dead marriage’s gentleness out of my own hands in aisle three, and I did not think about the man with the gift shop once, which I mention only because it is the last evening I can point to and say for certain that I did not.

The house on a school night had its particular sound, which was two eighteen-year-olds who no longer needed me for anything essential and required me for absolutely everything else. Sophie ran the household’s administration from the kitchen table without looking up, told me I’d said Tuesday about the form and it was now not Tuesday. Eli was on the third step from the bottom, the one place the whole downstairs can see him, which is where he goes when a thing is bothering him that he’s decided not to say, and I sat on the step above him with my knees at his shoulders, the old geometry, and did not ask, and after a while he leaned his weight back against my shins, which he would not have done in daylight in front of another person, and I rested a hand on the top of his head, no message in it, and he let me. That was the thing I was good at that nobody pays you for, knowing which child needs the words and which one needs you to have no words and just be the newel post they lean on. I made the dinner badly. We ate it. I checked the doors, front and back, the nightly liturgy of a woman making sure the people she loves are sealed in with her for the dark, and I looked in on the quiet of them and went to bed in a full house, tired in the good way, forty years old, certain, and safe.

I would like to tell you I saw something in that message. I have gone looking, since, for the version of myself who should have. She isn’t there. There is only a competent, tired, funny woman in a gold room who could read a two-in-the-morning text off a stranger’s phone and lay the whole mechanism bare, and who read eleven words about a gift shop on her own screen and saw a coincidence and a friend and a joke about soap, and put the mushrooms back for her son, and went home, and slept. I have spent my life being the one in the room who sees it. I want that understood before I tell you the rest, because the rest is the story of the one time I did not, and of how long, how astonishingly long, a person who diagnoses this for a living can go on not seeing the thing she could have named in anyone else inside of a single afternoon.

Chapter Two

If you had asked me that spring whether I was happy, I’d have said yes without having to think about it, which is either the surest sign of a good life or the surest sign you’ve stopped checking, and I genuinely could not tell you, even now, which one it was for me.

There were a hundred days left, and Sophie had put the number on the fridge. It lived under a magnet shaped like a lobster that had been on that door since before she could read, and she updated it every few days, ruthlessly, in the block letters she’s used since she was eight, because Sophie manages a feeling the way she manages everything, by turning it into administration. A hundred days until she and her brother stopped living in this house. She had come out of me first and impatient, twenty minutes ahead of Eli and a decade ahead of him in everything that involved leaving, built for departure, already half gone in the good way you are supposed to want and cannot quite forgive them for. She had a green grad dress hanging on her closet door that she would not let me fuss about and a spreadsheet for the residence packing that she would let me admire.

Eli was on the stairs. He does this, has since he was small, sits on the third step from the bottom, the one place the whole downstairs can see him, when something is bothering him that he has decided not to say. “Is it weird I’m not more excited,” he said, to the carpet. “About Western. Everyone’s so excited.” I sat on the step above him, which put my knees at his shoulders, an old geometry, and told him Josh was performing excited, that it was the season for it, and that he got to be exactly as excited as he actually was and nobody was reading his. He thought about that. “Okay,” he said, and went back up, and that was the whole of what he needed, which was to be told his own weather was allowed. I had two of them and I could read them in the dark, and whatever I later became, I could still do that, all the way through.

I had, that spring, what I can only call a full life, and I was aware of its fullness the way you are aware of a house you have finally finished paying for. The practice with the wait-list. The two good humans about to go be humans elsewhere. A profession that made me the person people called when their own lives came apart, so that my evenings were often other people’s emergencies and I was good at that, I liked being the one who knew where the exits were in every kind of room. And Priya, eleven years of Priya, who was the thing a marriage is supposed to be and often isn’t, the person who had seen me at my actual worst and stayed anyway.

She texted while I was still at the sink, and I dried my hands and went to it, easy, no weather in it.

Priya: wine thursday. you are not allowed to cancel, you have cancelled twice, I will come to your house with the bad orange stuff you pretend to like

Mara: I like the bad orange stuff completely unironically and that is the actual tragedy of me. Thursday. bringing the good cheese to apologize for the wine.

Priya I had known since she lived the floor below in a building where the walls were a suggestion, and complained about the crying coming through her ceiling, and then, one February so cold the tub taps froze, came up the back stairs with a lasagna and a bottle and never entirely left. She sang alto in a community choir that performed twice a year to rooms of politely suffering relatives and took it more seriously than anything else she did. She could not be lied to about the large things, only the small ones, because she had watched me become a person in bad light and knew the difference. That spring she was tired in a way she waved off, driving out to Mississauga more than she used to; her mother had left a pot on the burner until it scorched, twice now, and Priya told it as a joke, old people and their pots, and I laughed with her and let her move us on to the wine, because I was happy and busy and the moving-on was easy to allow, and I did not hear, under the joke about the pot, the first low note of a thing that would ask more of my friend that year than I was going to be there to help her carry. There was no coming year yet. There was a Thursday, and cheese, and a woman who loved me.

We still ate together on Sundays, the three of us, and that last spring it had quietly become less a habit than a thing we were refusing to stop doing. I cooked the chicken with the lemon, badly, the way I have made it badly for eighteen years, and it is theirs, the badness is the point. I stood over the stove while it did its work, stirring nothing, the spoon going round an empty pan out of some old need to be doing, and for no reason Noah was there, not there, the ghost of him, dancing. He used to dance at the stove, badly, deliberately badly, a big man doing a soft-shoe with a wooden spoon for a cane, to make the twins shriek and to make me roll my eyes and to make me, though he never said it and I never admitted it, look at him. I had not thought of it in years and then it was the whole kitchen, the spoon, the shriek, and then the timer went and I was back, alone at a stove nobody had danced at in a very long time. Sophie set the table without being asked, because she had entered her leaving-kindness phase, the one where the departing child turns briefly, unbearably thoughtful, and she folded the napkins the way my mother used to, into the shape my mother called a bishop’s hat, and she did not know she was doing an elegy. I did, and I said nothing, and I put the chicken down.

She carried the dinner, the way she does, filling the air so the rest of us can hide in it, and somewhere between the chicken and the clearing she told us about a boy she had decided against doing long-distance with. “It’s not even real,” she said, spearing a bean, eighteen and certain. “You’re not with a person, you’re with a little glowing rectangle that sometimes has their face in it. You fill in the rest yourself, the good version, and then you’re loyal to a thing you basically invented. No thank you.” I said that was wise. Something got into the word, a half-beat too much weight, and Sophie glanced up, quick, and I smoothed it before it landed anywhere, the way you smooth a tablecloth, and passed the beans, and the moment closed over and was gone. I did not sit there thinking about a screen of my own. It would be a tidier book if I had. I thought my daughter was sharp and a little hard and exactly ready to go, and I felt the ordinary ache of it, and I got up to deal with the plates.

Eli did the thing he does while we cleared, stood rinsing a plate longer than a plate needs, back to me, and said, “Do you think Dad’s sad. That we’re both going.” Not are you sad. He had decided mine was handled. It was his father he was carrying, out past the edge of the city in the town with the new cedar deck and the calm good woman named June who folds a program to the right page and brings iced tea from actual tea, and I came and stood beside him at the window and told him his dad played the long game, that he would miss them like a limb and be fine, that he would get a dog inside the year, watch. Eli laughed, wet, and I put my hand flat between his shoulder blades, and we stood in the going light, my anxious boy and I, and the size of loving him came up in me the way it does, too big for the kitchen, and I thought only that, only how much, with nothing attached to it yet, no ledger, no cost, just a mother at a sink with three months left of a full house and a heart that ached from being that full.

I went up when they had gone quiet, and on the counter, face down where I had left it right through dinner, was my phone, which had gone off once, softly, somewhere around the chicken. I had not turned it over. I had a rule about the kids’ nights, a rule I had made without ever announcing it to myself, which should have been the tell and was not. There was a man I had met in February who sometimes sent me things late, a clever man, a friend, a small warm reliable pleasure at the edge of a life that already had everything in it, and I left his message lying face down on the counter and went up to bed. If there was anything to notice in the fact that leaving it there took a small, specific, deliberate effort, the kind of effort you only ever spend on a thing you have decided not to look at, I did not notice it. I was happy. The house was full. I slept.
