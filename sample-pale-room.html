The Pale Room

A free sample: Chapters One and Two.

Chapter One

The therapist had told her to write the dream down, so she opened a new document and faced the white of the page. It came most nights now. It was always the same.

A white room, no corners. The walls ran on past the place where a wall was supposed to stop, washed out, the colour gone out of them, and the light came from no direction she could name. The air there had no temperature. Someone stood close behind her, and she could never turn far enough around to see who it was.

She typed a sentence and took it back off the page. Tried another. She deleted that one too, because nothing she put down held the real feel of the place. A photograph of a room is never the room. The dream wasn’t made of things she could list. It was how it felt to stand inside it, and that part went out of the words the second she wrote them.

She sat with her hands off the keys a while and let the cursor do its blinking without her. The therapist had made it sound simple. Put it on the page and it stops being yours. She’d been at the page a week of mornings now, and the dream still belonged to her down to the last white inch of it. The page took nothing.

The dream used to scare her for the whole rest of the day. It didn’t anymore. It came, and it passed, and she let it pass, and by the second coffee it was just a thing that had happened in the night.

After a while she gave up. She saved the handful of lines, none of them right, and closed the file.

The logo project opened to where she’d left it, which was nowhere. Her client ran a boutique hotel downtown, established 1947, and the brief wanted minimal and modern at once. Two words at war. The deadline was Friday and she had nothing.

She made small clean marks for other people’s businesses, and most days that was a living she could stand inside. Today it sat on her chest like a held breath.

She pulled up reference instead. Lobby shots, ceiling roses, brass gone green at the seams, the slow expensive hush of places she’d never stay in. One photo was a room-number plaque, 307 in a plain serif. She liked the brass and the clean cut of it, so she dropped it on the mood board and kept scrolling past more of the same, none of it work.

The scroll went on and she let it carry her. A dark wood desk. Then a bar under low gold light. A corridor of identical doors running back to a point. Somewhere in the mix a news result she hadn’t gone looking for, some downtown hotel and a missing-persons appeal she didn’t click, gone again by the next swipe. She read the rooms for the small choices in them, the weight of a font on a sign, the join where one material met another, and built a small wall of other people’s good taste between herself and the empty file. None of them would turn into a logo. She saved them anyway.

The monogram was the part that wouldn’t come. Its two initials were supposed to lock into one clean mark, and instead they fought her at every setting she tried. The kerning stayed wrong. A serif made it worse, a sans made it worse a different way, and after an hour the canvas was as empty as it had started.

Work used to arrive in a rush, whole, her hands scrambling to keep up, and she’d surface hours later with something real already there. These days the canvas stayed white while her own head went white to match it.

She set the cursor at the top of the canvas and left it there. The day went on outside without her. Cold came up through the floor into her socks, and she pulled her knees into the chair and stayed small, the empty file open in front of her where it had sat all morning. Her tea went cold. She got up once to run the tap hot over her wrists and came back with nothing decided, and the chair had kept her shape.

The coffee sat by her elbow in the white ceramic mug, the handle chipped. Still a little warm, so Lucas had set it down on his way out and she hadn’t registered him do it. Cream, one sugar, exactly right. Five years, and he’d never once gotten it wrong.

She clicked back to the logo file, where the cursor blinked at the spot the first mark should have gone. The clock read 11:47. She’d opened the file at 9:15, which put two hours behind her with nothing made inside them. She noted it and let it close over.

Her shoulder had gone stiff in the chair. She dug a thumb into the knot of it and held until the dull ache answered, more solid than the bright empty screen in front of her. The small pain was something. It was hers, at least, and it answered when she pressed. She let go, and the ache stayed a second, and then it was gone.

The phone buzzed once against the desk.

Morgan: Coffee this week? Haven’t seen you forever.

She answered without picking it up all the way.

Mina: Swamped with work. Soon though.

Three weeks now of Morgan asking and her saying soon. The word cost nothing and meant about that much. What Morgan wanted was a table out in the world somewhere, two coffees, a clear look at her across it. That was the trouble. One good look and the careful questions would start, and Mina didn’t have the answers ready out loud. She turned the phone face down.

The afternoon went the way the morning had. She meant to make lunch, and then it was past two and she hadn’t, and the hunger had thinned to a flat grey nothing. She still hadn’t worked. The light slid off her desk and onto the far wall while she wasn’t watching.

At some point there was a glass of water she didn’t finish, left sweating a ring onto the desk. The apartment kept its afternoon quiet around her, the fridge cycling on and off, a neighbour’s door somewhere down the hall. She sat back down. The chair held the dent of her by now.

The phone lit a few times through the afternoon and she let it lie. A parcel left with a neighbour, a sale at a shop she’d bought one thing from years ago. She turned each notice over without opening it and set the phone back down. None of it needed her. The square of light from the window had crossed the desk and reached the far wall and started up it by inches, and she found she’d been marking the day by where the light had got to and not by anything she’d made, because she’d made nothing.

At some point she was standing at the open fridge with the cold air spilling out over her bare feet. She’d come in for something. Whatever it was had gone. She closed the door on the lit shelves and stood there in the dark of the kitchen a little longer, then went back to the desk and the file that hadn’t changed.

She closed the file rather than keep looking into it, then opened it again a minute later because the not-looking was worse than the looking. The two letters sat there refusing each other, close and then far apart, a hair too heavy and then too thin. Nothing in it moved. She’d drawn clean marks for strangers for years, and this one wouldn’t give her the start of itself.

Lucas got in a little after three. He kissed the top of her head and asked how the logo was coming. She told him it was good, she was making progress. The screen said otherwise. He saw it and let it stand, and she pulled an old project into a fresh tab so the monitor would look busy.

He hadn’t always let it go. The first couple of years he’d have pushed, wanted the file open and the lost hours accounted for, and somewhere in the therapy that had gone out of him without her seeing it leave. Now he asked nothing he didn’t want answered. She missed the pushing sometimes. Some days she couldn’t tell his growth from a quieter way of giving up.

Five years of the table and the late teas, and the evenings barely changed from one to the next. He held up his side of it without seeming to try. She held up hers as best she could from where she was. It held.

They settled across from each other at the kitchen table. Or he worked, and she kept the mouse moving in small slow circles, a busy hand over a still file. At some point he crossed to the kettle and came back with a fresh Earl Grey by her elbow, the string hooked over the rim the way she took it. She watched the steam come up off it and thin into the lamplight. She didn’t drink it. The surface went still after a while and took the lamp in it, one small lit circle riding on the dark of the tea. He answered an email with one hand and reached across to straighten her coaster with the other, a small housekeeping of her done without looking up.

The lamp made a small pool of light on the table and let the rest of the kitchen go to dusk. A fly had got in earlier and was working the window over the sink, knocking the glass and resting and knocking again, and she followed the small dumb persistence of it instead of the file. It quit eventually. The room went back to the two of them and the quiet.

He typed across from her, the keys going in little runs and stops, and she set her own false work to the run of his real one. The bergamot rose off the tea and then quit. She let it. When she looked again the cup had gone cold and his own was drying by the sink, and she couldn’t have said how long the table had held them both like that.

A notification lit the phone. Her thumb went wrong on the glass and dropped her into contacts instead of the app, and she was halfway down the names before the wrong one landed. Maya (don’t call). A Toronto number under it she hadn’t dialled in longer than she let herself reckon, just the name and the warning she’d set in the brackets, an entry dragged through every phone she’d ever owned.

She didn’t call it. That was the arrangement, the one she’d made with herself and kept without much trouble most days. Her sister. There used to be no one closer, and now there was a closed door where that had been, and Mina stood on her own side of it and didn’t knock. Her throat tightened on the name. She left me too was the shape the old ache took, which wasn’t fair and was true anyway, and she didn’t follow it any further than that.

She backed out and put the phone face down.

Lucas made pasta and talked while it cooked, the work restructuring again, the same as last week. Jenkins was finally retiring and leaving the reporting lines in a mess, and there was a question now of who’d take the floor and whether it reached down as far as his team. He moved around the small kitchen while he said it, the wooden spoon, the colander, the salt held high over the pot. The sauce needed longer than he gave it, the way it did every time, and neither of them had said so in five years. She nodded on cue. The pasta was fine. She said so twice, and meant it both times, and couldn’t have named the shape of the pasta an hour later.

The list ran on. It went behind her eyes the whole time he spoke. Milk, the good coffee, the bin bags they were nearly out of, a card for someone whose name she’d lost. The steam off the pasta water fogged the window over the sink. His voice came through it even and warm, asking nothing of her that she had to answer yet, a weather she could stand in without being seen.

He worked his way through the whole of it, who’d be moved under whom and which of the old arrangements would hold and which wouldn’t, and she gave him the small sounds that kept it going, the hm and the right and the tilt of her head at the turns. The card, she thought. She’d meant to buy the card three days running now and lost it each time to something smaller. His fork turned the pasta and he ate between sentences, and the kitchen held the smell of garlic and the low even run of his voice and the window going dark behind the sink, and she sat inside all of it with her list and her quiet and a plate she couldn’t taste.

Then he asked what she thought, and she surfaced a second too late. “About what?” she said, and watched him set his fork down.

“I just spent five minutes explaining the whole thing.”

“Sorry. Just tired.”

He told her she was always tired lately. He let it go. He didn’t make it into anything the way he would have once, and she ate because the plate was in front of her and the hour called for it. The pasta had no taste she could find. She got it down anyway, fork to mouth on a borrowed rhythm, watching the small certain movements of his hands as he ate across from her.

He cleared the plates and ran the tap, and the ordinary clatter of it reached her from where she’d moved to the couch. She should have got up to help. She stayed where she was. The cushion had warmed under her, and moving felt like more than the help was worth.

Later they were on the couch together with the television going low. He reached for her and she let him, easy the way long practice is easy. It had been two weeks. The wanting was polite on both sides, and his hands knew the whole route after five years, her body answering the way it had been taught. His mouth found her neck and his weight settled over her and the cushion gave under them, and the television threw its changing light across the ceiling, and she let her eyes go up and track it.

She got there in the end, her mind on the grocery list the whole way through. The heat was real. He said he loved her and she said it back, and both things were true, and neither of them felt like anything she could hold.

Later she went to bed by herself, Lucas still somewhere in the apartment behind her. She lay on her back in the dark and looked up.

There’s a water stain over the bed, kidney-shaped. Yellowish brown, there since she moved in, the landlord promising and then not promising and then not mentioning it again. It stayed. She traced its edges with her eyes, the long curve of it, the narrow end, the way it bled at one side where the damp had spread and dried and spread again. She knew its outline by now, every lobe and inlet of it, a map she’d never sat down to study and had anyway.

The apartment was quiet. She could hear Lucas moving in the other room, the low sound of the television, the tap running once and going off. She lay under the stain and kept tracing it until the sounds stopped and the whole place went still around her.

At 3:07 AM she woke gasping, the red numbers of the clock the first thing she fixed on, and the dream came down on her all at once, the white room and the walls washed out and the drowning whiteness and someone standing close behind her she couldn’t turn toward, all of it crowding in on a single long line before it let go and left her in the dark with her own breathing.

She went to the kitchen. Ran the tap cold and drank a glass standing at the window. The street below was empty, the pavement lit orange under the lamps, nothing moving. She stood there until the glass was done and her breathing had come back to its usual pace, then rinsed the glass and set it on the rack and went back to bed.

Sleep didn’t come again. The apartment ticked and settled around her. She lay under the stain and watched the dark lighten by degrees, the kidney shape coming back out of the ceiling as the room grew grey, and she was still watching it when the alarm went at seven.

Lucas showered and dressed and came in to kiss her forehead, his coat already on. The door closed. The apartment went quiet again, just her and the sound of the street starting up outside, delivery doors and a dog somewhere and a car that wouldn’t turn over on the first try.

She opened the laptop. Found the file from yesterday morning, the handful of lines that hadn’t caught the real feel of the place. She read what she’d written and it was still wrong, still a list of the room’s facts without the thing that made the room the room. She sat with her hands off the keys and let the cursor blink and did not add anything to it.

Chapter Two

The coffee was on the nightstand when she woke, still warm, cream and one sugar. His way. He’d set it down and left her sleeping, the way he had every morning for months, the mug turned so the handle faced her hand.

A note leaned against the mug, and she read it before she let herself drink. Didn’t want to wake you. Have a good day. -L. Five years on, and he still got the cup right, the cream and the one sugar measured out exactly, like it cost him nothing to know her this well.

She drank it in bed. Too sweet, and warmer than it had any right to be. She finished it anyway, because he’d made it and left it where she’d find it.

The same flat light filled the bedroom, holding steady through the morning. The radiator ticked. Outside she couldn’t tell if it was going to rain or had already finished, the sky one sheet of nothing much from the eaves down.

She showered and dressed. The laptop went onto the kitchen table, and the logo file got exactly one look before she closed it again, the deadline a day closer, and she reached for the phone instead.

Morgan had sent the link months back, some astrology app with a whole brunch lecture about algorithms and birth charts. “You have to try this,” she’d said. “It’s uncomfortably accurate.” The thread had sat unread since, like everything did lately.

She opened it now. It wanted a birthday, then a birth time down to the minute. She didn’t have the minute, so she gave the same half-remembered number from an old document she’d lost. She uploaded a photo without checking which one.

The chart loaded as a wall of text she read maybe a third of. A rising sign. A square between two planets she’d never heard of, and a moon the app called difficult without saying why.

There was a line about an Anti-Vertex, fated encounters and karma and past lives, the sort Morgan would underline. The rest was paywalled, so she scrolled down to the matches.

The app put a number on each stranger, and she thumbed past them, sixty-one and fifty-three and sixty-eight. One at seventy-one. Then, near the bottom, one she stopped at, higher than the rest by a long way.

T.K., forty, Toronto. The photo was a distance shot, a man at an outdoor table, his face too far to read. Dark hair, and a bio one line long that said he was here for the people who think too much at 3am.

Ninety-four percent.

Under the number the app had put a line of its small serious type, calling the alignment “rare,” “potent,” the kind of match it said completed a pattern rather than made one, two charts that read less like strangers than like halves. She read the words the way she read all such words, and kept a little of them anyway.

She checked it twice in case the app had glitched. Then she scrolled back through the others to be sure she hadn’t imagined the gap between it and the rest. Sixty-eight was next. The number sat at the top like the screen had run out of room above it.

She should have worked. She typed instead, before she could think better of it.

Mina: 94%. That’s high.

The three dots came up before the screen had settled.

T.K.: Highest I’ve seen too. Two months on here, never above eighty.

Mina: No idea what it even means.

Mina: My friend Morgan would know. She’s the one into this.

T.K.: Should we find out?

She didn’t answer straight. She asked him something small instead, and he asked one back, and that was the whole of how it started. It didn’t feel like starting. It felt like picking something up partway through, a thread already running.

He did IT, he said. Database work for a hospital downtown, servers and tickets and people who deleted the thing themselves and swore they hadn’t. She did logos. Mostly for people who didn’t know what they wanted until they saw what they didn’t.

T.K.: So you read minds for a living.

Mina: I read minds people don’t know they have. They hate it, then they pay the invoice.

T.K.: What are you working on now?

Mina: Hotel rebrand. Boutique place, nothing fancy.

T.K.: Do you like it? The work.

Mina: Sometimes. When the client knows what they want, which is almost never.

T.K.: And you’re the opposite, I’d guess. Someone who always knows what she wants.

Mina: You’d think so. No. I haven’t known what I wanted from a Tuesday in years.

T.K.: Wrong, then. Noted.

He didn’t defend the guess. She liked that, a man who could put a theory down as soon as it stopped being true.

T.K.: Did you study it? The design.

Mina: No. English. Which is what people study when they want to be told they have taste.

Mina: I fell into the other thing after. It pays on time.

She sent it and it went the way those lines went, light, already finished with itself, and he took it lightly back, and neither of them stopped on it.

They talked about the small lies the work ran on, the that’ll-hold he typed while already planning the workaround. A blue logo she’d once called purple. The client loved it blue. He asked if the performing bothered her, and she told him she’d stopped noticing when she did it.

T.K.: When you lie?

Mina: When I perform.

The dots came and went on his side before he answered.

T.K.: Most people wouldn’t say that.

Mina: Maybe I’m not most people.

T.K.: No. You’re not.

T.K.: You stop noticing the performing because there’s a version of you underneath that never started. She’s the one I’m talking to.

She read that twice. It landed too square, named a thing she’d never put words to, and she told herself he’d guessed, that it was the kind of line that fit anyone if you squinted.

Mina: Lucky guess.

T.K.: Probably.

They got into worst jobs. He’d done eight months in a call centre and still heard the hold music in elevators sometimes. She’d worked a print shop once and told him about the day she’d backed into the big-format printer, knocked it into the wall, and stood there while a four-foot banner of the mayor’s face fed out slowly across the floor and came to rest over her shoes. He said that was the most he’d ever wanted to have been somewhere.

T.K.: We’re bonding over misery. That’s a sign of something.

Mina: Of two people who’ve had bad jobs.

T.K.: Romantic, when you put it that way.

They moved off work and onto movies, the way conversations do when they’re looking for more ground. He liked slow films, the kind where nothing happened for long stretches and then one small thing did and you understood the stretches had been the point. She liked horror. He found that interesting.

T.K.: Horror as in actually scared, or horror as in you like watching people make bad decisions.

Mina: Both. Mostly the second one.

T.K.: So you watch them for the comfort.

Mina: You watch someone walk into the basement and you already know, and they don’t, and somehow that’s the relaxing part.

T.K.: That’s a very specific kind of comfort.

Mina: It works.

He named a film from the nineties she hadn’t heard of, a man returning to a town he’d grown up in and finding everything the same size it had always been, just him changed.

Mina: That sounds unbearable.

T.K.: It is. That’s why it stays.

They drifted onto music next. Nobody steered it. He listened to things with long instrumental stretches and no lyrics to get in the way, which fit him. She had a playlist she put on when she worked that she’d never shown anyone, not even Lucas, because explaining it would mean explaining the mood it was for and she didn’t have words for that yet.

Mina: Music you listen to alone is different from music you’d play for someone.

T.K.: Yeah. It’s more honest.

Mina: You’d be embarrassed by mine.

T.K.: I doubt that.

Mina: It’s very sad.

T.K.: Mine too.

Mina: What do you listen to when you can’t sleep.

T.K.: Whatever doesn’t make me think too hard. Sometimes nothing. Sometimes the same album until morning.

Mina: Which one.

T.K.: I’ll tell you when I know you better.

Mina: Fair.

It went on like that, quick and easy, each answer making room for the next one before she’d finished it. She kept waiting for the lull where a stranger runs out of things to say. It never came. She got up once for water and drank it at the sink, looking at the courtyard, the same brick and the same square of grey sky, and she didn’t know how long she stood there with the glass going warm in her hand before she made herself go back.

Her phone lit in her hand, Nasser’s name across the top, the buzz of it cutting into the thread. She thumbed it silent. The deciding never quite happened, and she kept her eyes on what T.K. had just sent. A few seconds later a text dropped down where the call had been.

Nasser: You coming Sunday.

She left it there and went back to the thread. Nasser would have an answer for the chart, probably, or a joke about it, the way he had a place for everything she brought him. He’d been the one to fold her into all of it, the Sunday tables and the weddings and the funerals where everyone knew everyone, and Sunday was Sunday and she would go or she wouldn’t. Sunday could keep. She set the phone’s buzz out of her mind and watched the dots start up on T.K.’s side.

She told him she was always performing, that she’d forgotten how to stop. He didn’t think she was. She asked how he could possibly know a thing like that about a stranger he had never met.

T.K.: Because you keep saying you don’t know. Nobody performs not knowing.

T.K.: Can I ask you something real?

Mina: You’ve been asking real things for an hour.

T.K.: Fair. Do you believe in this? The chart, the number.

Mina: No. You?

T.K.: No. But I’m still here.

Mina: Same.

He asked about her family, and she told him about her mother, which she hadn’t planned to do. It came out the way a folded thing opens, once you finally stop holding it shut.

Her mother said twenty minutes. Going to the store, twenty minutes, the door already half open behind her, the cardigan blue and the sneakers white. Mina was nine, and she’d believed it, because twenty minutes was a number you could count down and wait out.

Mina: My dad waited at the kitchen table with me. Past dinner. Past the news. Until midnight.

Mina: Then he said she wasn’t coming back.

He’d made tea around nine, two cups, because nine was the hour her mother usually wanted one. He set hers across. He didn’t drink it, and he kept his hands around his own long after it sat there cold. Her chair he never moved, and it stayed pulled out a little, on her side, for years.

She could still see the stove clock from where she’d sat, the long hand and the slow one. She’d watched both move and learned that twenty minutes had no real edge to it, that a number could be a thing someone handed you to hold while they left.

There’d been a letter, she found out later, left where her father would find it and not her. She never read it. By the time she was old enough to ask for it, it was gone, and asking only made the room go quiet in a way she’d learned to leave alone.

It was Maya who took it over after that, all of it, though she was only fourteen herself. Five years older, acting forty, packing the lunches and signing the school forms in a careful copy of a grown woman’s hand, sitting on the edge of the bed on the nights the dark wouldn’t settle and telling her the same three jokes until she laughed. She raised her. Their father worked doubles and her sister did the rest, and nobody ever decided it out loud, and then four years later his heart stopped between one shift and the next and there was nobody to decide it with. Maya was eighteen. She did not go where she’d been going.

She got work instead, and kept getting it, and what she made went on the table and into Mina’s school fees and later into the tuition, and she did that for eleven years without once saying it was a thing she’d done.

There was a night Mina had never handed to anyone, not Lucas, not the therapist with her gentle questions. Sixteen, a school trip due at seven the next morning and the fee gone missing from where it should have been, and Maya coming in past midnight in her work polo with her name tag still pinned crooked, counting bills out of her own pocket onto the kitchen table without being asked, not looking up, saying only, go to bed, I’ve got it, in a voice too tired to be anything but true. Mina never once heard her complain about money in front of her. She only found out later, going through boxes, that some months there hadn’t quite been enough of it, and Maya had made the going-without look like nothing at all.

Maya braided her hair every morning before school, three over and under, fingers quick, never once pulling. Mina had loved her the uncomplicated way you love the person who stays.

The table got left behind when they packed up the place, the chair still pulled out on her mother’s side. She’d forgotten it until tonight. Now she could see the grain of it, the worn spot where a hand went to drag it back, the whole thing she’d never once told Lucas in five years of telling him things.

She’d given Lucas a version of the rest once, flat, like reading off a file. This was more. Her throat was tight and her eyes hot by the time she’d sent the whole of it to a stranger.

Mina: My older sister did most of the raising after that. Maya. She was fourteen herself.

T.K.: That stays with a person.

Mina: It was a long time ago.

T.K.: Doesn’t make it not matter.

She waited. There was a beat on his side, the kind where a person decides something. She half expected the next question, the obvious one, the where-is-she-now that anyone else would have sent. He didn’t. He moved on the way you do when you already know the room and don’t need the tour.

His mother she asked after carefully, leaving room around the question.

T.K.: We called her Rania. She had these eyes. You’d meet them once and not forget.

She asked what she’d been like. He gave her a sentence that stopped before it landed, the quiet way people go around their dead. She let it lie. They moved back to smaller things.

Lucas came home while she was still in it, the door and the keys in the bowl and his voice asking how the day had gone. Fine, she said, making progress, and he believed it the easy way he believed her when she sounded like herself. She turned the phone face-down.

He made dinner the way he always did, and she didn’t watch a single part of him doing it. The food arrived plated. He talked while they ate, something at work that had gone sideways and come right again, a name retiring, a migration pushed to next week, and she made the sounds that go in the gaps and tasted little of what was in front of her. The screen lit under her hand between his sentences. She picked it up and set it back down whenever he glanced over, and he glanced over less than he used to.

Afterwards he went to the corner desk and put the loupe in his eye and bent over the flea-market watch he was bringing back, and the small sounds of it reached her from the couch, tweezers on the tray, a part set down on felt, the little screwdriver finding its slot. He could sit like that for two hours and want nothing from anyone.

“You’ve been on that phone all night,” he said. Not an accusation, just set down gently, the way he set everything down now.

“Work thing.” It came out easy, the way they all came out easy now, and she watched herself say it. He said he was turning in. She said a while longer. He went, and the apartment quieted, and she stayed at the table with the screen bright against the dark window.

They kept going. He asked a married-sounding question, light, and she stepped around it. When she asked him the same back, he stepped around it too, and neither of them minded, two people agreeing without a word to leave the furniture of their lives outside the door. The room she should have been in was one wall away, and she stayed where she was.

They talked about fate the way two people do when they don’t believe in it, whether you could be fated to something you walked into by accident, whether the accident still counted if you’d chosen it.

T.K.: Can I ask you something else.

Mina: Go ahead.

T.K.: Do you think we’ve met before.

Mina: I don’t know. You feel familiar.

T.K.: Yeah. You do too.

The light in the kitchen hadn’t moved, or she hadn’t been watching it move while the hours slid past. Usually they just went. This time she’d been inside every one of them.

It was 11:11 when she looked up. She stared at the number the way she had as a child, and made a wish without meaning to, and a second later couldn’t remember what it was.

Mina: I should go.

T.K.: Yeah. Me too.

Neither of them closed the app. She watched the two minutes pass, the thread sitting open between them.

Mina: Goodnight.

T.K.: Goodnight.

She plugged the phone in. Getting ready for bed took longer than it needed to. Lucas was already asleep, his back to her side, the duvet pulled to his shoulder. She lay down beside him in the dark, close enough to feel the warmth of him, and looked at the ceiling.

Ninety-four percent. You feel familiar.

The numbers on the clock moved. She didn’t. She lay there with the thought of T.K. in the room the way a song gets in and won’t clear, not painful, just present, a frequency she couldn’t tune out. The two phrases kept circling, the number and the line, the number sitting highest the way it had on the screen. She’d told a stranger about the chair her father never moved, told it before she’d told it to herself, and in the dark it would not lie down. It had been easier with him than with the man one wall away who got her coffee right every morning of a marriage she could mostly no longer feel.

She told herself it was a tired thing, a whole day spent talking to a stranger, nothing more than that. People did it. She believed it for about a minute at a time, then lost it, then found it again. She turned onto her side. Lucas’s breathing went on, steady and even, the whole length of him turned away, and she listened to it until somewhere around one she stopped being awake.

In the morning the phone was the first thing, before she was properly awake. One message waited at the top of the thread, sent at 3:07 AM.

T.K.: Can’t sleep. Keep thinking about our conversation.

She’d been asleep at 3:07, flat asleep, the kind with no edges to it. The watch agreed. She looked at the timestamp twice, the same way she’d looked at the ninety-four twice, making sure she had it right. He’d been awake, thinking about her, while she was somewhere with no edges at all.

She scrolled up through the night before answering, the whole thread from the first message to the last. It read like old friends. None of it squared with the plain fact that yesterday morning he had been nobody to her. She’d dreamed again, too, the white room and the washed-out walls and someone behind her she couldn’t turn to see, and she didn’t type that to him. She let it stay where the night had left it.

Mina: I was asleep at 3. Just seeing this.

T.K.: Good. One of us should have been.

Mina: You said Toronto. On your profile.

T.K.: Yeah. You?

Mina: Same.

The three dots came up and held there a beat longer than usual.

T.K.: Same city. This whole time.

She read it again. The app had shown her a distance shot, an outdoor table, a city that could have been anywhere. She’d assumed some other city without asking.

Mina: That’s either a coincidence.

T.K.: Or fate. If you believe in that.

Mina: Starting to.
