The Last Thing She Remembered

A free sample: Chapters One to Three.

Chapter One

The whiteboard by the door had her name on it in dry-erase marker, SOBOL, and under it the date, and under that the name of the nurse who had her until seven. Nadia read all three every time she surfaced, the way she used to read the caption line before a witness was sworn, and the reading steadied her the way it always had, because a name and a date and a person responsible for you were three facts, and three facts in a row were a record, and a record was a place to stand.

It was the fifth morning. She knew that not because she felt five days of it in her body, though she did, a soreness that had moved from her throat down into the muscles of her shoulders and back as if the thing done to her neck had been done to all of her, but because the board had been wiped and rewritten four times that she’d been awake to see, and each wiping was a day, and she had counted them. She counted most things now. She had caught herself at three in the morning counting the holes in the ceiling tile, thousands of them, and had made herself stop, because she knew what the counting was, the mind reaching for anything with an order to it, a banister in the dark, and she did not like to watch herself reach for it.

Her throat was the color of a plum gone soft. She had seen it on the second day in the bathroom mirror, one hand on the IV pole for the small distance between the bed and the sink, chin lifted under the strip light: the ring of it, thumb-shaped on the left side, four smaller ovals stepping around toward the back where the fingers had gone, and above the ring a scatter of red pinpricks like something flicked off a wet brush. Petechiae, one resident had said to another across her bed on the first clear day, both of them reading her chart and not her face, the way you read a chart. Burst capillaries, the second one had said, from the pressure. It tells you how hard and how long.

And Nadia had lain still and taken the word and kept it, petechiae, because a word was a thing you could hold, it stayed the same size in your hand, it did not get better or worse in the night.

They had explained the rest to her in pieces, when she asked, because she always asked. The strangulation had cut the blood to her brain long enough to put her out and long enough to take something with it when it went. Not the memory of her life. She had all of that, her mother’s maiden name and the smell of the courthouse elevator and the exact feel of her machine under her hands. What it had taken was a window, the last few minutes before the dark, the way a power cut takes whatever you hadn’t yet saved. The brain had been without air, a doctor named Adeyemi would tell her later in a clean office by the bay, and a brain without air does not always come back with the same furniture in the same rooms. For now it was only the window.

Five minutes, they thought, give or take, and Nadia lay in the tilted bed and thought that five minutes was nothing, that you could lose five minutes standing in line at the pharmacy, and that she would have given a year off the far end of her life to have those particular five back, and that the wanting them was its own kind of injury, one no chart had a word for.

She knew the shape of the days behind the window because she had built them back, brick by brick, the only way she trusted anything. The twenty-sixth of December, a Tuesday, the courthouse closed for the storm and closed again the day after, the whole city stopped under a snow that had not stopped for four days and would not stop for two more. She had gone in for her materials. She could give you the cold of the key fob through her glove and the particular dark of the parking deck at five in the afternoon with the lake throwing snow sideways through the open sides of the structure, orange where it crossed the one sodium light still working on the second level. She could give you the sound, somewhere below her, of a plow blade dragging concrete, a sound she had felt in her teeth.

She could give you her car twenty feet off, already a white mound losing its shape. And then the record thinned, the way a tape runs to its end, and there was a place where it should have kept going and did not, and after that place there was nothing, and after the nothing there was here, the tilted bed and the whiteboard and the plum-dark ring around her throat.

“You’re awake for real this time.” The nurse came in with the little paper cup of pills and a chart she was already reading. Renata, the board said, until seven. She was broad through the shoulders and unhurried in a way that read as competence rather than slowness, and she talked to Nadia like a person, which not all of them did; some of them talked to the chart, or to the injury, or to the sad idea of a woman who’d been choked in a garage. Renata talked to the woman in the bed. “Doctor wants you sitting up more today, and walking to the window and back twice before I’m off. You want the bed up, or you want to fight me on it.”

“Up is fine.”

“Smart girl.” She thumbed the control and the bed folded Nadia gently toward the window, and the movement pulled at the sore ladder of her back, and she breathed through it the way she’d learned to in five days. Outside, the city was buried to the sills. The plows had cut one lane down the middle of the street and heaped the rest into a wall taller than the parked cars, so the cars were only humps now, a row of white barrows, and the sky over them had the flat pearl glow it got when there was more snow in it and it was only deciding when. “Sixty-eight inches since Christmas Eve,” Renata said, following her look, hanging a fresh bag. “They’re saying it’s a record for the state. My road still isn’t touched. Yours either, probably, wherever you keep yourself.”

“East side. Off Parade.”

“Then no.” She said it kindly, checking the line, writing the number off the pump onto the flowsheet clipped to the rail. Everything she did she wrote down, and Nadia watched her do it with the old dumb comfort of it, a person leaving a trail so the next person would know what was true, the whole quiet architecture of care resting on somebody bothering to keep the record. “Nobody’s getting over to the east side till the weekend, hon. You picked a bad week to need somebody to come to you.”

Nadia turned that over and set it down without letting it show on her face, which was a thing she was good at, keeping the face still while the meaning arrived behind it. Nobody’s getting to you. It was true for the whole city and it had been true for her, in the deck, in the five o’clock dark with the lake unloading and the plow two floors down and no one, no one at all, coming up the unplowed ramp. She filed the fact where she filed the others, next to the cold fob and the dragging blade, in the row that ran up to the edge of the window and stopped.

“There was a detective,” she said. “Petrucci. He came.”

“Day before yesterday. He’ll be back, they always come back.” Renata capped her pen against the rail. “He seemed decent, for police. He didn’t lean on you. Some of them lean.”

“I don’t have much to give him to lean on.”

“You’ve got time to have more. Things come back.”

“Not this.” She heard the flatness in it and gentled the edge, because Renata had been kind and kindness should be met. “The memory. It’s not a drawer that fills back up on its own. They already told me how it works. The part that’s gone was never really written down. There’s nothing to come back to.”

Renata paused at the rail, the pen still in her hand, and looked at her, not at the chart. “They tell you a lot of things,” she said. “I’ve been on this floor eleven years. I’ve seen people get pieces back a year on. In the shower, driving, folding a shirt. You’d be surprised what surfaces when you stop dredging for it.” She straightened the blanket, a thing that didn’t need doing. “Do you remember any of it? The man? Sometimes it’s not the picture that stays, it’s a smell, a voice.”

There it was, the word, handed to her across the rail the way everyone would hand it to her for months, gentle, expectant, holding the door open for her to walk through. Remember. She held it and did not use it back.

“I can tell you the last thing that was written down,” Nadia said. “Four fifty-one in the afternoon, the twenty-second of December. I read the time into the record because the judge asked me for it, the way they do before a recess. Four fifty-one, off the record, court adjourned. That’s the last thing I’ve got that I know is true, because I made it true, I typed it while it was happening with my own hands.” Her throat tightened around the length of the sentence and she felt the plum-soft ache of using it too long, and she stopped and let it settle. “After that it’s the weekend, the storm, the twenty-sixth, the deck. Up to a point. Then it’s five minutes I can’t get to. Then it’s here.”

“Four days between,” Renata said, working it out. “The court thing was the twenty-second, and the garage was the twenty-sixth.”

“Four days.” Nadia looked at the buried street. “The last time everything was accounted for was four days before anything happened to me. I keep going back to it. Four fifty-one. It’s the last clean edge I’ve got.”

Renata didn’t try to fix that, which Nadia respected more than she could have said, and on her way out she reminded her the bed alarm was on and to ring before she tried the bathroom alone, and Nadia said she would and had no intention of it, and then she was alone with the radiator ticking and the pearl light going gray at the edges the way it did here in the afternoon, early, before five, the sun gone down behind the lake without ever having properly come up.

Her phone was in the bedside drawer with her two rings and the paper bag they’d cut her sweater into when she came in. She worked it out one-handed and the screen woke to a gray column of messages, days of them, the county courthouse contacts and her sister and two numbers she didn’t know, and she read them from the bottom up, in order, because order was the only thing that turned a stack of messages into a record instead of a noise.

Wendy: call me the second you can. i keep calling the nurse station, they’ll only say stable.

Wendy: stable is good. i looked it up. stable is good. i’m coming the second they open 79, i don’t care what it takes.

Wendy: bringing you real clothes and the shampoo you like. don’t argue with me about the shampoo.

She didn’t argue. She set the phone face down on her thigh and looked at the thousands of holes in the ceiling tile and did not count them.

She had been a court reporter for eleven years. Before that a medical transcriptionist for four, and before that, further back than she looked most days, the girl in the house who wrote things down, who kept a spiral notebook under the mattress and put the date at the top of every entry the way she’d seen her mother’s lawyer do, because she had learned young and hard that a thing which happened and was not written down could be said, later, by an adult with a calm voice, to have not happened, or to have happened differently, or to have happened only in the head of a child who was, everyone agreed, so imaginative. She did not look at that straight on.

She knew it was there the way you know the sun is there without staring at it, and she had built a whole working life at the exact profitable distance from it, close enough for the warmth, far enough not to burn, a woman who sat at the edge of the loudest rooms in the county and caught every word and kept none, so that what stayed in her hands at the end of a day was cleaner than what stayed in anyone else’s, uncolored, unwished-for, true. The record did not editorialize. The record did not lie awake. It had seemed to her, until the twenty-sixth, that the edge of the room was the safest place a person could possibly stand.

Her machine was somewhere in this building or in an evidence locker, and her notes with it, and she wanted them the way you want a hand to hold in the dark. She had asked about them on the third day, as soon as she was clear enough to ask, and Colleen had said not to worry, that she’d keep everything safe, and the worry had gone out of Nadia all at once, because it was Colleen, because Colleen had taught her before Nadia could brief a single word that the paper outlives the room and everyone in it. Between the two of them there was forty years of writing down what other people would have preferred to forget. If anyone alive would keep a record safe, it was Colleen.

The light in the window went from gray to the blue-gray that came early here in the last week of the year, and Nadia lay in the tilted bed and did the thing she did now in the place of the thing she couldn’t. She built it forward from the last true stroke. Four fifty-one, off the record, court adjourned, the twenty-second. The courtroom emptying, the lawyers zipping their bags, the accused led out the side. The weekend. The storm coming in off the water Christmas Eve and not stopping. The twenty-sixth, the fob, the east stairwell because the elevator was dark, the second level because that was where the reporters parked, always had, the snow sideways through the open side, the plow dragging concrete two floors down.

And then the place where the record should have been and wasn’t. And the one thing about that place she had not said to Renata and would not say to Petrucci, because she had no fact to hang it on and she did not deal in anything else. Her tape would be intact. She knew her own hands, she knew her own machine. Whatever had been done to her in those five minutes, the machine had been in her bag against her hip the whole time, closed, silent, keeping nothing.

The tape had no gap in it. She was the gap.

Chapter Two

The exam came before any of it, on the first night, while she was still a body and not yet a case.

They did it in a room off the emergency floor that had been made gentle in the way institutions make a thing gentle, a lamp instead of the overhead, a chair that was almost a chair you would have at home, a box of tissues placed where a hand would find it, and none of it fooled her for a second, because she had spent eleven years in rooms that had been arranged to hold what people could not hold and she knew the grammar of them the way she knew her own hands.

A nurse named Delacroix ran it, a broad calm woman past fifty who had plainly run it a thousand times and had learned to narrate every step before she took it, I’m going to touch your jaw now, I’m going to ask you to turn your head toward the wall, tell me if you want me to stop, and Nadia lay on the paper and turned her head toward the wall and answered the questions in the flat exact voice she used for a record, because the flat exact voice was the only room in her that the deck had not gotten into.

There were photographs. The camera made its small mechanical sound, the same sound the courtroom’s old evidence camera made, and Delacroix moved the lamp and lifted Nadia’s chin with two fingers and photographed the throat from the left and the right and straight on, the bruising already coming up out of the skin the way a print comes up in a tray, faint and then not faint, a shape that would be plum by morning and yellow by the second week of January and gone, mostly, by the time anyone would want to see it.

Petechiae, Delacroix said to the resident who was charting, and spelled it, and Nadia, flat on the paper with her head turned to the wall, spelled it too, silently, P-E-T-E, catching the word the way she caught every word, because a word you have caught is a word that cannot later be said to you as though you had never heard it.

She understood, lying there, more of it than they thought she did. She understood that the marks on her throat were being made into a record, a second record, one written in her own skin by a stranger’s careful hands, and that this record and the one she could not reach, the five dark minutes, were now the two ends of the same missing thing, and that everyone in her life from this night forward was going to spend their time trying to make the second reach across to the first, and she understood, before the exam was even finished, that they would not manage it, because she had already been reaching all night and there was nothing on the far side of the gap to take her hand.

Wendy came through the storm. She should not have made it and she made it, forty minutes to drive the six miles from the east side with the plows already beaten, and she came into the gentle room still in her coat with the snow not yet melted on her shoulders and stopped in the doorway, and Nadia watched her sister’s face do the thing it had done exactly once before in their lives, in a different doorway, a long time ago, in a house neither of them named, the face going white and then deliberately, forcibly, calm, the older sister putting her own horror away somewhere fast because there was a younger one on the table who could not be asked to carry it. Wendy did not cry.

Wendy came and stood by the head of the table and put her hand flat on the crown of Nadia’s head, the one place on her that did not hurt, and left it there while Delacroix finished, and said nothing, because the two of them had learned before they were grown that there are rooms where the only true kindness is to not make the hurt one manage your feelings on top of her own.

“I’m here,” Wendy said finally, when it was done, when Delacroix had stepped out and it was just the two of them and the lamp. “I’m not going anywhere. You don’t have to talk.”

“I can’t tell them what he looked like.” It came out of Nadia flat and level, a fact entered into a record. “I have everything else. I have the whole day. I have the walk to the deck and the cold and the light being orange and the sound of the plow two floors down. And then there’s a piece missing and it’s exactly the piece they want and I don’t have it, and I keep reaching for it, Wen, I keep reaching for it the way you reach for a step that isn’t there in the dark, and my foot keeps coming down on nothing.”

“Then it comes down on nothing.” Wendy’s hand did not move from her head. “You don’t owe the nothing anything. You hear me. You are not the missing part. You’re everything that’s still here.”

It was, Nadia would think much later, the truest thing anyone said to her in the whole of that winter, and it was said in the first hour of it, by the one person who had been in the first house with her, and she was not ready to hear it, and she filed it anyway, where the true things went, to be understood on a night at the end of April when the lake had finally let go of its ice.

Chapter Three

The first time they asked her, really asked her, was in the hospital on the third or fourth day, when the swelling in her throat had gone down enough to talk and the doctors had decided her mind was clear enough to be a witness, and a detective who was not yet Petrucci, a younger one, kind and out of his depth, sat by the bed with a recorder and a notebook and walked her toward the gap, and she went, and fell in, in front of him, and could not get out.

She had been fine, at first. Better than fine, she had been herself, because they started at the far end, the safe end, and she gave it to them the way she gave everything, exact and ordered and complete. The trial recessing. The storm. The plaza and the wind. The stairwell, the wire glass, the snow going sideways. The orange light and the cars gone to hulks and her own car down at the end. She heard the detective’s pen going and knew, from the sound of it, that he thought this was going well, that she was a good witness, the best kind, a professional, a woman who caught the world for a living and was catching it now for him.

She had felt, for those few minutes, almost like herself, almost useful, and had not yet understood what was coming, that she was walking him and herself straight at the lip at a confident pace.

And then he said, gently, and the man, can you tell me about the man, and she reached for it, and there was nothing there, and the nothing was not like forgetting, she would try for months to explain this and never manage it, the nothing was not a fog she could strain to see through, it was an edge, a clean bright edge past which there was no her at all, and she reached for it again, harder, the way her whole life had trained her to reach for the next thing, the next word, the thing just said, and her reaching hand closed on nothing, closed on the turning spool, and she heard her own voice say I don’t, I can’t, it’s not, and stop, and start, and stop, and she watched the young detective’s face change from the pleased attention of a man taking a good statement to the careful blankness of a man watching a witness come apart, and she understood, lying there, that she had just become, in his notebook, in his recorder, in the official first record of the thing, a woman who could give you everything up to the exact point that mattered and then nothing, nothing at all, and that there was no worse kind of witness in the world, that a witness who has all of it except the one thing is worse than a witness who has none of it, because the having-all-the-rest is what makes the missing part look like a door she is choosing not to open.

“It’s all right,”

the detective said, which was the wrong thing, the kind thing and the wrong thing, and closed his notebook a little, and she saw him close it, saw the small withdrawal of a man deciding a witness had given him what she was going to give, and she lay in the bed after he left and understood the shape of the rest of her life in that one closing notebook, that she was going to spend it walking people to the edge of the thing they wanted and watching their faces change when she could not step off it, and that the wanting in their faces, the small held breath before the gap and the small withdrawal after, was going to be the thing she could not bear, more than the fear, more than the pain, the specific loneliness of being the locked room everyone believed had the answer and being, herself, on the near side of the door, unable to open it, and blamed for it, gently, in a closing notebook, forever.

That was the cruelty no one intended and everyone committed. Not one of them meant to blame her. The young detective was kind. Petrucci, later, was kind. Wendy, the doctors, all of them kind, and the kindness was its own fine blade, because the kindness was the sound a person makes when they have privately concluded that you are holding something back and have decided, generously, not to press you on it. She could hear it in the softening of their voices, the it’s-all-right, the take-your-time, the we-can-come-back-to-it, and she knew exactly what it was, because she had heard it once before, long ago, in a kitchen, from a woman with a clipboard who had been kind too, kind right up until she closed the folder, kind in the exact voice people use on a person whose account they have stopped believing.

She had spent thirty years and a whole career making sure no one would ever use that voice on her again, building herself into the one person in any room whose account could not be doubted, and the deck had undone all of it in five minutes, had put her back in the kitchen, back under the clipboard, a person being kindly disbelieved, and she understood in the hospital bed that this, not the pain and not the fear, was the thing she was going to have to survive, the return of the voice, the soft kind sound of not being believed.

She did not cry then either. She had not yet cried and would not for a long time. She lay very still and did the only thing she had ever known how to do with a thing she could not bear, which was to begin, silently, to keep the record of it, the date, the detective’s name, the exact words she had managed before the edge, so that at least the account of her own failure would be true and complete and hers, since the thing the account was about never would be.
