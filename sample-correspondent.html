The Correspondent

Chapter One

The Kessler bundle came in badly scanned, which Isolde had expected, and out of order, which she had not.

She worked at the table under the window. The window gave onto the brick of the next building, close enough that in summer she could hear other people's radios through it, and in March it gave her nothing but a flat gray rectangle that meant the lamp stayed on all day. Four days in. Nine hundred and forty pages of exhibits from a construction dispute in Guelph, and somebody at the other firm had fed the whole thing through a document feeder that skewed every third sheet by about four degrees, so half the page numbers sat crooked in the corner like they were sliding off.

She built the chronology in a spreadsheet. One row per document, columns for the date on the face of it, the date she thought it was really from, the source, and a column headed WHY that nobody ever read except her. The rows were the part of the job she liked. Everything else was typing, and the typing was what she got paid for, and she had made a kind of peace with that around the second year.

The apartment was two rooms and a galley kitchen and she had been in it six years. The radiator under the window knocked twice when the heating came on and once when it went off, and she had stopped hearing it, and then had started hearing it again in the winter when the second bundle dried up and she was in the apartment fourteen hours a day. You could hear the stairs from the table. It was the only way she ever knew anyone was in the building.

At eleven she found the first bad date.

Exhibit 208, a site meeting minute, dated the fourteenth of June. The witness statement said the meeting where the drainage was raised had happened before the pour, and the pour was the eleventh. She put the two documents side by side on the screen, moved the minute to the left because that was the way her eye ran, and read it again.

The minute was a typed-up version of somebody's notebook. You could tell from the way the attendance list ran, four names and then two more squeezed on the end in a different rhythm, which meant the two had come in late and been added by hand and then transcribed as though they had been there from the start.

"The meeting where drainage was raised," Isolde said, "was the second week of June, before the concrete went in. I remember it because my daughter's birthday is the tenth and I had to leave early."

She was looking at exhibit 208 while she said it. The witness statement was four hundred pages back in the bundle and she had read it on Monday afternoon. She got it word for word, including the and at the start of the last clause, which was the kind of thing that dropped out of a paraphrase and which she had never once had to work at holding. It did not occur to her to remark on this. She had been doing it since she was nine and had assumed for a long time that everyone could, and by the time she found out otherwise it was too late for it to feel like anything but a slightly embarrassing party trick that had no parties to go to.

So the fourteenth was wrong. Somebody had transcribed a handwritten four from a two, or the notebook had said 12 and the typist had read 14, which happened more often than anybody would believe.

She flagged it, wrote the note in the WHY column, and found the second one an hour later in a delivery docket that had a fuel surcharge on it for a route that did not exist yet. Two corrections in a morning was a good morning. Some weeks she went the whole week and found nothing except her own eyes going.

Trish emailed at one.

have you got room for another one after kessler, it's smaller, mostly emails, would need it by the 4th, say no if you're drowning

Isolde typed yes and looked at it and deleted it, because a one-word answer looked like a woman with nothing on, and wrote yes, send it whenever suits, I'll be clear Friday. She read that one twice before she sent it.

At four she drove to Hamilton to drop a drive at the client's office, because they were the kind of firm that still would not put nine hundred pages on a server and still had a partner who said the word cloud with an audible space around it. The receptionist took the envelope without looking up from her screen. Isolde said thank you to the top of her head and went back out through the revolving door into the parking lot.

That was the second time she had spoken aloud since Sunday. The first had been to an empty room, about a woman's daughter's birthday.

The 401 westbound at half past five was solid to Milton. Her phone had been playing something that morning and it had run out and moved on to whatever came next in the queue, which was a true-crime series she had not chosen and had not turned off, three episodes deep by now, because the voices were company and she did not have to follow them.

The presenter had the voice they all have, the one that puts a little dip in before a proper noun.

She was saying that the thing about the Rowe case, the thing people still could not get past, was that there was nothing there. No connection. No grievance. Nothing that came out at trial and nothing that has come out since, and it is not for want of looking, because in the first two years there were four people looking full time.

"She stood at the end of the driveway," the presenter said, "and she looked at three people, and she picked one."

Isolde turned the volume up.

The name was Vesper Vale. She was twenty-four. The woman she killed was Dianne Rowe, fifty-two, a facilities administrator walking to her car on a Saturday morning in April, and the street was residential and ordinary and there had been nine people on it. Vale did not run. She sat down on the curb about forty feet away and waited, and when they took her in she gave her name and then said nothing at all, and had gone on saying nothing at all through a trial and seven years of a life sentence.

The co-host said the thing about how she must have wanted to be caught.

The presenter said maybe. Then she said the other thing, which was that in seven years Vesper Vale had never once said she was sorry and had never once said she was innocent, and that either of those on its own was unusual, and that having both at the same time was almost unheard of, because the two of them together left a person with nothing to say to anybody. There was a silence on the recording where the co-host did not have a follow-up.

The traffic moved eleven car lengths and stopped again. Somebody four cars up had their hazards on for no reason anyone could see.

Isolde thought about the file. Not about the woman, and not about the street, which she had no picture of and did not want one. About the file: seven years of a person declining to produce the one document that costs nothing, which is a decision made fresh every day for seven years, and about how nobody in the episode seemed to have found that interesting. The presenter had said she must have wanted to be caught in the voice of a person who had never in her life read a document that disagreed with her.

She got home at seven. She ate standing up at the counter, a bowl of the thing she made on Sundays, and did not sit down until she sat down at the table, and then she did exhibits to page four hundred and stopped because her eyes had gone.

At ten she got out paper.

She wrote about the bundle first. She knew it was ridiculous while she was doing it and kept going, because the alternative was to write about the crime and there was no version of that which was not obscene. She wrote about the two dates and the transcribed four and the fuel surcharge. She wrote that she had heard an episode about her and that the episode was badly made, that it had a music sting under the sentence about the curb, and that she was not writing because of the offense and had no interest in it, and did not want anything and had nothing to offer.

She wrote that she had noticed Vale had never said she was sorry. She wrote that she had noticed people found this offensive, and that she thought people found it offensive because an apology is the cheapest object in the world, and refusing to produce one looks like arrogance when it might be the opposite.

She wrote that she was thirty-one, that she indexed evidence for lawyers, and that most of what she knew about people she had learned by reading their emails after their lives had already gone wrong.

Five pages. She put them in an envelope, wrote the institution's address off her phone and the name above it, and went down three flights in her coat over what she slept in.

The box was at the corner by the laundromat. Cold and wet under the streetlight, the sidewalk shining, the vents behind her pushing out that smell of hot lint that stayed in the street all night. She lifted the flap and put the envelope in and let go of it, and heard it land on whatever was already down there.

Then she went back up and did another sixty pages and slept eight hours, which she had not done since the previous Tuesday.

Chapter Two

Niamh had two clients she did not worry about. Isolde Mercer was one of them, and had been for about eighteen months, and the eighteen months were the part Niamh was pleased with, because the two and a half years before them had been very hard indeed.

She came in at four, second Thursday, and sat in the chair she always sat in, and put her coat over the arm instead of hanging it up, which she also always did. Four years of a coat over the arm of a chair. Niamh had stopped offering the hook in the first month.

"Good week?"

"Good three weeks."

"Tell me the good."

The work first. Isolde always started there. Two bundles, one finished ahead, a new client through Trish that might turn into something steady. She said the word steady twice. Niamh let it go the first time and picked it up the second.

"Steady's a big word for you."

"It's a normal word."

"For you it's a big one."

Isolde smiled at the carpet. She had a way of conceding a point that involved not looking at anybody, and it had taken Niamh about a year to stop reading it as sulking and another year to work out that it was the opposite, that it was what she did when she had been pleased and did not want to be watched being pleased.

They did the mother. Peggy had had a bad couple of weeks in February and a better March. The home wanted a form signed that needed two signatures, one from each daughter, and Isolde had signed hers and posted it and heard nothing back. She did not say the other name. Niamh did not say it either. Four years in, that was a place they went to on purpose and never by accident, and the rule about it had been made in the first year for good reasons that still applied.

They did the sleeping. It was better and had been since about November. They did the drinking, which had never been a problem and which Isolde raised herself roughly twice a year in the manner of a woman pressing a bruise to check it is still only a bruise.

Niamh wrote three lines while she talked.

She wrote them the way she had written notes for twenty-seven years, which was with the client's own words inside quotation marks and nothing of her own inside them. Paraphrase was where the tell went missing. If a woman said I was fine about it and you wrote down client reported equanimity, you had thrown away the only useful thing in the sentence, which was that she had reached for the word fine, in that tense, out of the twenty better words available to her, and had reached for it fast. Six months later the note would tell you she had been calm. It would not tell you she had been quick.

So the note for that hour had "steady" in it twice, and "a good three weeks", and "I signed mine."

There was a thing about this client that made the method easy, and Niamh had noticed it in the first month and had put it down as a pleasantness. Isolde brought other people's sentences in whole. Not the sense of them and not a version of them, the sentences themselves: her sister's, a solicitor's, a woman at a bus stop, delivered flat and in the original order with the bad grammar left in. Niamh had assumed at first that she was tidying them on the way, the way everybody does, and had tested it once without meaning to, on a line of her mother's that came round again three months later, and it came back identical both times down to a the that had no business being in it. She had written client quotes at length and accurately in the file that week and had never written it again, because it was not a symptom of anything and there was nothing to be done with it. It made the notes better. She had four years of a woman's exact words and had never once had to ask her to say a thing twice.

At ten to five she raised the conference, and they moved the next appointment out by a week, and Isolde stood and put her coat on properly for the corridor. She had her hand on the door handle when she said it.

"I wrote to somebody. A letter, on paper. It was a stupid thing to do and I feel fine about it."

Niamh had a policy about the door as well.

It was not written down and she had never explained it to a client, because explaining it would have made it a rule the client could work with. Whatever arrived in the last thirty seconds of an hour had arrived there for a reason, and the reason was nearly always that there was no time left for it to be asked about. Taking it up anyway meant running the session past the hour, in a corridor, standing, with the next person already in the waiting room hearing every third word. She had done that twice in her twenties. Both times had gone badly, and one of them had gone badly for a year.

"Next time," she said. "Bring it back and we'll do it properly."

"There's nothing to do properly."

"Then it'll be a short conversation."

Isolde laughed and went out. Niamh heard her say something friendly to Marc in the corridor and heard Marc laugh, that big unembarrassed laugh he had, and heard the outer door.

She wrote the last line of the note. "I wrote to somebody. A letter, on paper." Quotation marks, verbatim, because it was the method and the method did not have an exception for lines that sounded like nothing.

Marc came in at ten past with two coffees and sat on the arm of the client chair, which she had asked him not to do for eleven years.

"I want to talk about the thing I keep not talking about."

"Sell your half."

"End of next year." He put her coffee down on the wrong side of the desk and she moved it. "I want to sell my half by the end of next year and do two days a week somewhere I don't have to think about the boiler, or the parking, or whether the woman who does the invoices is going to retire."

"She is going to retire."

"That is exactly my point."

She was surprised by how much she minded, and then not surprised, because she had known since the autumn and had been letting herself not know, which was a thing she would have named in a client inside four minutes.

"You'll want a valuation."

"I'll want a valuation." He drank some of his coffee. "And a clean two years. That's the honest bit. Nobody buys into a practice with an open complaint on it, or a college letter, or anything the insurer has a file on. It doesn't matter how it ends up. It matters that it's open when they're looking at the books. Boring is the whole product."

"Nothing here is exciting."

"That," said Marc, "is what I am counting on."

She washed the cups and shut the machine down and locked up at six. It was still light, barely. The parking lot smelled of wet gravel and somebody's cigarette from the office block behind, and she sat for a minute before starting the engine, which she did most days and had never once examined.
